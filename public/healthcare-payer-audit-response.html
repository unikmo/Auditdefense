HEALTHCARE PAYER AUDIT RESPONSE

Build the factual record before choosing the response.

A payer audit can combine documentation, coding, authorization, enrollment, network and payment findings in one spreadsheet. The first job is to separate them and connect every allegation to the correct claim and source record.

Organize my audit →Attorney workflow
What a response workspace should show
  • the exact claim universe and dates of service;
  • initial and later payer decisions;
  • evidence supplied at each stage;
  • unresolved factual conflicts; and
  • questions reserved for counsel.

What should happen first after a payer audit notice?

Preserve the notice, attachments and date received. Record the stated deadline exactly as written and mark it unverified until the controlling contract, policy or program rule is reviewed. Do not reduce the matter to a single “denied” label: the same claim may carry separate findings for documentation, provider enrollment, authorization or coding.

AuditDefend organizes facts and source material. A qualified healthcare attorney determines legal interpretation, response strategy and any deadline on which the provider will rely.

The five-part audit record

1. Demand and scope

Payer, audit type, stated amount, review period, claim population, sampled claims and cited authority.

2. Claim ledger

Member-safe identifier, claim number, date of service, codes, units, billed amount, paid amount and provider identifiers.

3. Finding history

Initial decision, rebuttal submission, later decision, issue category and the reviewer’s stated reason.

4. Evidence map

Where each note, plan, assessment, authorization, signature record or enrollment record is maintained.

5. Source register

The exact contract, payer manual, state rule or program source—with version and effective-date context.

Counsel questions

Unresolved issues presented as questions, not automated legal conclusions or predicted outcomes.

A claim-by-claim workflow

Define the claim universe.Reconcile the demand letter, worksheet and payment data so omitted, duplicated or out-of-period claims are visible.
Separate every finding.Classify documentation, enrollment, authorization, coding, credentialing and network issues independently.
Point to the supporting record.The provider identifies where the original record is held and which version existed when the claim was submitted or reviewed.
Compare allegation with evidence.Record whether the current evidence appears to address the stated factual issue, while leaving legal sufficiency to counsel.
Preserve the response trail.Keep the submission, confirmation, payer reply and later decision connected to the same claim.

Common audit findings that should remain separate

  • missing or incomplete session documentation;
  • signature, attestation or date concerns;
  • treatment-plan or assessment timing;
  • authorization period, units or service-code questions;
  • billing or rendering NPI enrollment on the date of service;
  • credentialing, network or group affiliation;
  • coding, modifiers, units and place of service; and
  • calculation, sampling or extrapolation methodology.

A provider may possess evidence that addresses one finding while another finding remains unresolved. Combining the issues can hide both the available support and the remaining gap.

What the attorney receives

The attorney should receive a source-linked chronology, claim matrix, demand reconciliation, evidence gaps and a list of legal questions. The workspace may identify factual inconsistencies—for example, a record marked “unsigned” when the located version appears signed—but it should not state that a rebuttal is legally justified or forecast how a payer, agency or court will decide.
